Analysis · official records, whole country

Burned, then built?

There is a stronger version of the suspicion than the one usually tested, and it deserves testing properly: that big fires are set, as the first step of a development plan, so the cleared land can be built on. That claim makes a prediction sharp enough to check against public records. We checked it. It does not hold — and this page says so, because the case for the Gardunha does not need it.

What we found

The pattern is not in the data

Result

Solar plants are built further from recently burned land than chance would put them — not closer. And the big fires that solar does eventually follow are less often deliberate than the fires it never follows, not more.

The claim

Stated as strongly as it deserves

The weak version says developers take advantage of land that happens to have burned. That is unremarkable, and hard to object to. The strong version says the burning is arranged — that the fire is lit deliberately, before the land can be acquired, by or for whoever intends to build there.

Only the strong version is worth testing, and it predicts two things. Solar should cluster near land that big fires cleared a few years earlier. And those particular fires should be attributed to deliberate ignition more often than fires elsewhere. Both are measurable against ICNF's fire record and DGEG's licensing register.

Test 1 · proximity

Is solar built near land that recently burned?

For every solar plant with a known build year we asked whether a big fire had burned within a given distance in the years before. Then — and this is the half that matters — we compared it against how much Portuguese land in general sat near a recent big scar in that same year. Without that comparison the test is worthless: Portuguese solar boomed after 2018, so "built after a fire" is nearly free.

Rather than pick one definition and defend it, we ran every reasonable one: two fire-size thresholds, three lookback windows, four radii, and two independent plant samples kept separate instead of merged. Forty-eight combinations.

A ratio below 1 was always likely for an innocent reason: fires burn steep upland scrub, solar wants flat accessible ground. So the ratio alone settles nothing.

The permutation test is immune to that. It keeps every plant exactly where it is and shuffles only the build years — two thousand times per configuration — asking whether the real timing beats a random one. Terrain cancels out completely. Nothing came close to significance. The lowest p-value across all 48 was 0,23, which is what noise produces across that many tests. The p-values in fact cluster near 1,00, meaning the true build years placed plants further from recent fires than most random reshuffles managed.

Test 2 · attribution

Are the fires solar follows more often deliberate?

ICNF assigns a cause to almost every fire it maps, deliberate ignition included. That turns the question into something much closer to the actual allegation: among big fires, are the ones later followed by nearby solar disproportionately arson?

One cell does lean the claim's way: at 10 km with a five-year window, fires followed by solar are 60,0% arson against 47,3%. It does not survive inspection. It is one of twelve tests, its p-value is 0,15, and its own neighbours contradict it — tighten the radius to 5 km and it flips to −18 points, widen the window to ten years and it collapses to +1,5. A signal that appears only at 10 km is also a strange shape for the hypothesis. Burning land ten kilometres from where you build is not a plan.

What the record does show

Three findings that survive

A null result on one claim is not a clean bill of health for the landscape. Three things in the same data hold up, and two of them are serious.

Deliberate ignition doubles its share among the big fires. 22,4% of all fires against 46,6% of those over 1 000 ha — 454 048 hectares. Someone is lighting the large ones. What the record does not say is who: "imputável" is a legal category covering land clearing, grudges and pyromania alike, and it names no beneficiary.

Wind sits on land that burns at 2,42× the national rate. Not because anyone chose scars — measured against its own neighbourhood wind is 0,98× — but because turbines take unpopulated ridge scrub, and ridge scrub is what burns. The timing settles the direction: 115 parks burned after they were built, against 2 before. This is the finding that matters for the Gardunha, and it runs opposite to the burn-to-build story. Putting infrastructure on those ridges puts it in the path of the fire.

Individual cases remain, as cases. They do not generalise into a pattern, but they are real and worth naming.

Limits

What this cannot tell you

  • Roughly half of Portugal's solar plants carry no build date in either register. The proximity test runs on 86 and 170 plants — enough to detect a strong nationwide pattern, not enough to catch a handful of individual cases.
  • The date available is commissioning, not siting. A plant switched on in 2023 was sited around 2019, so short lookback windows credit fires that arrived after every decision had been made. That is why the analysis reports 3, 5 and 10-year windows rather than one.
  • Correlation in space and time cannot separate arranged burning from opportunism, from cheap marginal land, or from coincidence. Even a positive result would not have named anyone.
  • Absence of a fingerprint in the geography is not proof that nothing ever happened. It means that if it happens, it is not happening at a scale this data can see.
Conclusion

Why publish a result that goes against us

Because the case against covering the Gardunha in turbines does not need this claim, and is weakened by carrying it. The measured facts are strong enough on their own: wind occupies precisely the ground that burns, at nearly two and a half times the national rate, and the fires arrive after the parks are built rather than before.

Attaching an unproven arson allegation to that would hand the other side the easiest possible rebuttal — and when it fell, it would take the solid findings down with it. We tested the claim we would most have liked to be able to make. It did not hold. That is worth saying out loud.
